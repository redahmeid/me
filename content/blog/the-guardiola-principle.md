*[Read part 1 - Stop thinking about strategy as war](/blog/stop-thinking-about-strategy-as-war)*

If you want a single figure who embodies what strategic thinking looks like when it borrows from sport rather than war, it is Pep Guardiola.

Not because he wins - though he does, with a consistency that borders on the unreasonable. But because how he thinks about winning is so instructive and so transferable, it deserves more attention from technology leaders than it typically gets. He has no war room. He has no enemy to destroy. He has a game to understand, a system to build, and people to develop. That framing alone puts him closer to the best technology strategists I have worked with than any military theorist I have read.

## Four Lessons from Guardiola

### 1. The system must be felt, not just followed

Guardiola's foundational idea is *juego de posición* - positional play. It is not a formation. It is a philosophy about how space and time create advantage. Every player must understand not just where to stand but why that position creates the conditions for everything else to work.

This is the difference between handing an engineering team a reference architecture and building a culture where engineers genuinely understand why certain principles exist. Reference architectures get ignored under pressure. Principles that are understood get adapted under pressure. If your team can't explain the reasoning behind your strategic technical choices, you don't have a strategy - you have a document.

And here is the paradox that most people miss: the more rigorous the system, the more freely the individuals within it can express themselves. De Bruyne's improvisational brilliance, Silva's instinctive movement, Foden's creativity in tight spaces - none of it happens in spite of the structure. It happens because of it. When the basics are automatic - where to stand, when to press, how to recycle possession - the mind is freed for the genuinely creative decisions. Guardiola doesn't constrain his players' creativity. He creates the conditions in which it can reliably appear.

This is one of the most underappreciated ideas in technology leadership. Cognitive load is the enemy of creativity. When engineers are spending mental energy on slow pipelines, unclear ownership, fragile deployments, and undifferentiated infrastructure work, there is nothing left for the problems that actually require original thinking. Platform engineering, well-defined team boundaries, clear architectural principles, the right tools - these are not bureaucratic constraints on talented people. They are the structure that sets talented people free. Done well, they do for your engineers what Guardiola's system does for his players: they make the hard things automatic, so that the genuinely creative work can begin.

### 2. Every element of the system must be in harmony

Guardiola has a plan for the first corner of the game. A plan for the first wide free-kick. When he arrived at Bayern he noticed the grass on the pitch was too long, so asked for it to be mowed shorter. He has been known to straighten the dictaphones in front of him before a press conference. There is a famous video of him showing Raheem Sterling how to get in the right body shape to take the ball on the run, rather than turn.

None of this is disconnected obsession. Every detail exists in service of a single coherent system. The gridded training pitches reinforce positional play. The goalkeeper who plays out from the back is the first node in a possession structure that extends to the final third. The inverted full-back is the link between defence and midfield that makes the whole shape function. Pull any one element out and the others are weakened. As Piqué put it: "Pep doesn't just give you orders. He also explains why." Each player understands not just their role but how their role connects to every other. The principle stays constant. Everything must be in harmony or nothing quite works.

Technology organisations fail this test more often than they admit. You can articulate the most compelling engineering culture - autonomy, continuous delivery, psychological safety - but if the architecture is a tightly coupled monolith where every change is slow and risky, the culture will buckle under its own promises. If governance requires six sign-offs for a routine decision, you haven't built autonomy into the system - you've named it as a value while designing it out of the operating model. If team topology forces engineers to coordinate across three other teams to ship a single feature, the organisational structure is working against the engineering strategy, regardless of what it says on the slide deck.

The question worth asking is the same one Guardiola asks before every season: does every element of the environment reinforce the same principles? Does the architecture enable what the culture promises? Does governance serve the engineering or constrain it? Do the tools match the level of craft you are asking for? And as a technology leader, are you concerned enough with how code reviews are done, or is that detail you don’t need to worry about?

If the answer to any of those is no, you don't have a strategy problem. You have a harmony problem. And you cannot coach your way out of it.

### 3. Reinvent when you're winning - not when you have to

Guardiola won the Bundesliga in each of his three seasons at Bayern. He won six Premier League titles at City, including four in a row. And across that entire period he never once waited for results to dip before changing the system. Repositioned full-backs, abandoned the false nine, rebuilt entirely around Haaland, questioned the box midfield while it was still delivering titles. Each reinvention came not from crisis but from reading, while still winning, where the current approach would eventually be stopped.

The principle stayed constant. The expression of it changed every season.

Most technology organisations do the precise opposite. They find something that works, institutionalise it, and call it best practice - until the monolith nobody will touch, the pipeline that takes three days, or the data model that no longer fits the business makes the decision for them. By then, change happens under duress, against resistance that has had years to harden. This is precisely why at least 70% (and according to some analysts as high as 88%) of digital transformations fail. Because the culture of change is anathema to “successful” organisations.

"Works fine" is not a reason not to change. For Guardiola, it is the trigger for change. The organisations that build that same instinct into their culture - that treat good performance as the signal to evolve rather than the justification to stand still - are the ones still competitive a decade later.

### 4. The half-time interval is a strategic reset

There is a well-documented pattern with Guardiola's teams: they frequently emerge for the second half with a fundamentally different shape or pressing trigger, having identified something in the first forty-five minutes that changes the approach entirely. The interval is not a rest. It is an active strategic moment.

This is what your quarterly strategy review (or reflection breakpoints) should look like - and rarely does. Not a reporting exercise where teams present RAG statuses against pre-agreed targets, but a genuine inflexion point where leaders ask: what has the last ninety days told us that we didn't know when we started? What is the game revealing about our assumptions? What needs to change before the second half begins?

## A Different Kind of Strategist

There is a moment in youth football that I have witnessed dozens of times on pitches across Berkshire on Saturday mornings, and it never loses its clarity.

A twelve-year-old receives the ball under pressure. Everything they have practised - the body shape, the first touch, the scan before the ball arrives - either holds or it doesn't. No coach can intervene. No tactic sheet helps them now. What carries them through is the understanding they have internalised, the confidence built through repetition, and the trust they have in the players around them. In that moment, strategy and execution are the same thing. The big picture and the one percent are inseparable.

That is exactly what technology strategy feels like from the inside, when it is working.

The war analogy was never really about war. It was borrowed because strategy needed a vocabulary that sounded serious. Sun Tzu, Clausewitz and Boyd offered that vocabulary at a time when nothing else was readily available. Their contributions are genuine. I am not suggesting we stop reading them.

But the vocabulary we borrow shapes the questions we ask. And the questions the war analogy tends to generate - who is the enemy, how do we defeat them, what territory must we hold - are increasingly the wrong questions for the strategic challenges most technology leaders actually face.

This is not about sanitising strategy or pretending that competition isn't fierce and consequential. It is about choosing a frame that is more honest about how technology markets actually work - iterative, relational, adaptive, and ongoing. Nobody wins a war and comes back next season to do it again. In sport, that is the entire point.

Guardiola will not win the Premier League every year. The All Blacks will lose test matches. Sport does not promise unbroken victory - it promises a framework for sustained striving, for learning through competition, for building something that is greater than any individual contribution and more durable than any single result.

> Wars end. Seasons continue. The best technology organisations I have worked with understand this instinctively - they are not trying to win a conflict, they are trying to build a team good enough to compete, season after season, in a game that never really stops.

The language we choose to describe strategy is not neutral. It shapes what we see, what we value, who we include, and what we think success looks like. For too long, the dominant language has been one of conquest and annihilation, borrowed from a human activity defined by its worst outcomes.

Sport is defined by its best ones. The last-minute winner. The comeback from impossible odds. The young player who finally puts it all together. The team that was written off and came back stronger. The coach who found a way to make eleven individuals play like a single, elegant idea.

We have spent long enough thinking about strategy as war.

**It is time to think about it as the beautiful game.**
