For most of the second half of my career, I’ve thought deeply about what strategy means in the context of technology. I’ve spent a lot of time leading technology teams and advising companies through moments of ambition and crisis.

I’m also a massive sports fan (more coaching, watching and thinking about it these days than actually playing - though golf and padel still match my pace).

Somewhere in the overlap between those two worlds, I've come to believe that sport gives us a far richer, more honest, and ultimately more useful mental models and vocabulary for strategy than the one most of us inherited.

Because the one we inherited is built on war.

Before I explain why sport offers a better frame, let me be honest about why I find the war analogy problematic. It does so on two levels - one moral, one practical - and I think both matter.

## The Moral Concern

Let's be clear from the start, when making a moral case, it is naturally personal. But I think the case is still there to be made.

Strategy’s language is built on war. Our strategy thought leaders based their thinking on war. And it makes sense because humans are often at their most creative at times of adversity. Rarely is there anything more adversarial than war.

However, the military tradition from which our strategic vocabulary descends was built by men, for men, in service of conquest. Its virtues are hierarchy, sacrifice, discipline, and the subordination of the individual to the command structure. Its ultimate metric is the capacity to destroy and subjugate.

When we borrow that vocabulary - when we talk about campaigns and war rooms and killing the competition and deploying our people - we are not just reaching for a convenient metaphor. We are importing a set of values and assumptions that sit poorly with almost everything the best technology organisations are trying to build, and the world in which I want to live.

We want to build psychologically safe teams, yet we hold our strategy sessions in war rooms. We discuss servant leadership, then frame our competitive position in terms of enemies and territory. We talk about inclusion, then reach for a tradition that, for most of its history, excluded more than half of humanity by design. It is a language that requires us to hold our noses and accept the stench of destruction and exclusion.

That is not to say that Sun Tzu's understanding of asymmetry or Boyd's emphasis on speed in decision-making are not useful ideas in strategic discourse. I constantly use Boyd’s OODA loop for framing how we make decisions, and I will not stop.

But the frame in which those ideas arrived - the frame of enemies, annihilation, and zero-sum conflict - carries costs that we have been too slow to reckon with.

## The Practical Concern

Even if you set the moral question entirely aside, the war analogy is a poor fit for how business and technology strategy actually work. It is not just that the metaphor is uncomfortable - it is that it produces the wrong thinking.

War is structured around a set of conditions that simply don’t apply to most walks of life, be that technology or business. It assumes a defined enemy. It assumes a finite conflict with a clear end state. It assumes that the goal is the elimination or surrender of the opposing force. It assumes that resources are the primary determinants of outcome. And it assumes that the losses suffered along the way are acceptable costs in service of eventual total victory.

None of that maps cleanly onto the reality of building and leading a technology organisation. Your competitors are not enemies to be destroyed. Markets are not territories to be seized and held. And the people you lead are not troops and resources to be deployed - they are the primary source of your strategic advantage.

The war analogy produces thinking that is adversarial when it should be systemic, finite when it should be adaptive, zero-sum when markets are often positive-sum, and destructive when the task is generative. It frames strategy as something that ends in victory or defeat, when in reality, the best technology organisations are engaged in something much more like an ongoing season than a campaign. There is no armistice. There is only the next match.

Sport asks different questions entirely. And I feel those questions are much closer to the ones technology strategy actually requires.

## Four Reasons Sport Is a Better Analogy

### 1. Defeat is not destruction

In war, losing can mean annihilation - territory seized, armies disbanded, nations erased, generations displaced. The stakes are existential and the losses permanent. Sport understands defeat very differently. You lose the match, you learn from it, you come back next week.

This idea was never best illustrated than in the commencement speech of the great Roger Federer at Dartmouth University in 2024. “In the 1526 singles matches I played in my career, I won almost 80% of those matches”, he tells the crowd. Quite the boast. He then asks the crowd what percentage of points did he win in those matches. “Only 54%. In other words, even top-ranked tennis players win barely half of the points they play”, he told the fresh-faced graduates.

Defeats and setbacks are part of sport, part of life and part of business. Top athletes understand that a setback “is only a point”. And unlike war, setbacks are not the difference between life and death; they are simply lessons. There is always another point to play.

Technology organisations that treat setbacks as data points - rather than as existential threats requiring a war room response - in my experience consistently outperform those that don't. Commit to every point, every action and plan, but once it’s behind you, you move onto the next point.

### 2. Competition doesn't require elimination

War ends when one side surrenders or is destroyed. Sport ends in a scoreline - and then you go again. The Premier League needs Leeds as much as it needs Arsenal. A compelling title race requires genuine competition. The league's collective value depends on the quality of everyone in it.

Sport intrinsically understands this. Tiger Woods’ success made every golfer richer. Arrigo Sacchi showed the world why pressing worked, and Arsene Wenger would talk to journalists about healthy eating for player longevity. Muhammad Ali was not just the original GOAT but an icon that turned all sport into entertainment.

In sport, like in tech, competitors are not your enemy. They drive you to be better. Making others better makes you better. AWS pioneered the “cloud” because they told everyone about it. If others were not there to compete with them we may still be building our own servers, or at best using AWS’s EC2 instances. The “Spotify Model” only became a thing because Spotify presented it to the world. And while even Spotify no longer apply the model, sharing the principles a key element in improving software delivery. Netflix were not shy in telling the world about their approach to microservices.

### 3. Inclusion is a performance strategy, not a values exercise

The military tradition that gave us our strategic vocabulary was built on uniformity. Standardisation of thought, of appearance, of behaviour - because in combat, deviation from the plan costs lives. The ideal soldier is interchangeable, reliable, and obedient to the system. Individuality is a liability. The unit matters. The individual does not.

That logic made sense in its original context. Imported into technology organisations, it becomes quietly toxic. Teams where everyone thinks the same way, comes from the same background, and has absorbed the same assumptions build products with the same blind spots. They are optimised for internal coherence and terrible at representing the world they are building for.

Sport found this out the hard way - and then found its way to something better. The NBA's transformation in the 1970s and 1980s is one of the most instructive stories in the history of competitive performance. When black players began entering the league in significant numbers, they didn't just join an existing system. They changed it. They brought a style of play - improvisational, expressive, physically dynamic - that the game had never seen at scale. Rather than asking those players to conform to the existing template, the NBA had the collective wisdom to let the game evolve around what they brought. The result was the most exciting, commercially successful, globally compelling version of basketball the sport had ever produced. Diversity of approach didn't dilute the product. It transformed it.

The lesson is not simply that inclusion is morally right - though it is. It is that homogeneity is a strategic ceiling. The organisations that build genuinely diverse teams - in background, in cognitive style, in lived experience - consistently produce work with greater range, fewer blind spots, and more resonance with the people they are trying to serve. Broadening who gets to play, and building systems where different kinds of people can genuinely excel, is not a distraction from competitive strategy. It is the strategy. And while sport has some way to go still, it is leading the way in showing that diversity is a strategic advantage.

### 4. Sport is aspirational, not just adversarial

War is a story of the stronger defeating the weaker, or the better-resourced outlasting the less so. Its logic runs downward: find the opponent's weakness and exploit it, concentrate force where resistance is least, grind down what you cannot overpower. The strategic imagination it cultivates is fundamentally about destruction of the other, not elevation of the self.

Sport tells a different story. At its best, it is a story of individuals and teams in permanent pursuit of a better version of themselves, regardless of the opponent in front of them. The opponent is not the point. The opponent is the occasion.

Roger Federer never played a match simply to beat the person across the net. He played to express a version of tennis that nobody had seen before - fluid, precise, almost architectural in its construction of points. Rafael Nadal pushed him to levels he would never have reached alone. Their rivalry didn't produce one winner and one loser. It produced the two greatest players the sport has ever seen (in my opinion), each forged by the relentless aspiration the other demanded.

Even more so, grassroots sport understands this instinctively. The best youth coaches - and I have encountered both the good and bad on weekend matches - are not the ones who teach children to win. They are the ones who teach children to want to be better next week than they were this week. The scoreline is almost irrelevant. What matters is whether the player who couldn't receive under pressure in September can do it in February. Whether the team that defended in a panic in autumn can hold its shape in spring. The aspiration is continuous and it is personal. The opponent merely sets the context.

This maps directly onto how the best technology organisations think about growth. The question is not simply "are we ahead of the competition?" - that is a war question, and a dangerously lazy one. A company can be ahead of every competitor and still be stagnating, still be losing the best engineers, still be building products that are incrementally less relevant to the world. The better question is the sporting one: are we better than we were? Are our engineers growing? Is our architecture more capable than it was eighteen months ago? Is the product closer to what it could be, not just further ahead of what the competition has managed? The competitor sets the tempo. The aspiration sets the direction.
